Running the Numbers on a Rental Property

By Rob Vettese · November 26, 2026 · 3 min read

As an investor myself, I look at every rental property through a financial lens before anything else. Here's the basic framework I use with clients who are considering their first or next income property.

1. Start with Realistic Rent

Base your numbers on what similar units nearby are actually renting for, not the best-case figure in a listing. If the property is already tenanted, review the current leases, since existing rents may be below market.

2. List Every Expense

Common ongoing costs include:

  • Mortgage payments (principal and interest)
  • Property taxes
  • Insurance (landlord policies cost more than a standard homeowner policy)
  • Utilities you'll pay as the owner
  • Maintenance and repairs
  • Property management, if you won't manage it yourself
  • Condo fees, if applicable

It's also wise to set aside money for larger items like a roof, furnace or windows, even if they aren't due yet.

3. Plan for Vacancy

Even good properties sit empty between tenants. Building a vacancy allowance into your numbers gives you a cushion so one empty month doesn't turn a good investment into a stressful one.

4. Calculate Cash Flow

Cash flow is your rental income minus all of your expenses, including the mortgage. Here's a simple illustrative example, not based on a real property:

  • Monthly rent: $3,000
  • Vacancy allowance: −$150
  • Mortgage, taxes, insurance, maintenance and other costs: −$2,700
  • Monthly cash flow: $150

Positive cash flow means the property pays for itself with something left over. Some investors accept lower cash flow in exchange for expected long-term growth, but it's important to make that choice knowingly.

5. Look for Value-Add Potential

Some properties offer ways to increase income, such as updating units, improving the layout, or adding a secondary suite where local zoning allows it. These projects take planning and budget, so get proper advice on permits and costs before you count on them.

6. Screen Tenants Carefully

A great tenant is one of the most important parts of a successful rental. Consistent screening, including references, income verification and credit checks, helps protect your investment. Make sure your process follows Ontario's Residential Tenancies Act and human rights rules.

This is a general framework, not financial or tax advice. If you're looking at an income property in Niagara, Hamilton, Burlington or the GTA, I'm happy to run the numbers with you.
